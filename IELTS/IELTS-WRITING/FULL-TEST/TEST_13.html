A5. Model Report — Band 7.5+

The two pie charts compare the percentages of energy generated from six different sources in a country in 1985 and 2003.

Overall, oil remained the dominant energy source in both years, although its share declined substantially. By contrast, natural gas and coal became more important, while nuclear power recorded a marked fall; hydro was unchanged and other renewables increased slightly.

In 1985, oil accounted for 52% of energy production, far exceeding every other source. Nuclear power ranked second at 22%, followed by natural gas at 13% and coal at 8%. By 2003, the proportion supplied by oil had fallen by 13 percentage points to 39%. Meanwhile, natural gas rose to 23%, and coal almost tripled to 22%, meaning that the latter nearly matched the figure for gas. Consequently, the combined share of these three fossil fuels increased from 73% to 84%.

The remaining sources all represented relatively small proportions in 2003. Nuclear energy recorded the largest decline, dropping from 22% to 8%. Hydro remained unchanged at 4%, whereas other renewables rose from just 1% to 4%, reaching an equal proportion to hydro.

B4. Model Essay — Band 7.5+

Violent crime is frequently reported in newspapers and television programmes, and such coverage can influence both public attitudes and offender behaviour. Although I accept that sensational reporting may spread unnecessary fear and reward criminals with attention, I largely disagree with a complete ban because carefully regulated coverage serves important public purposes.

There are legitimate reasons to restrict the way violent offences are presented. Repeated headlines, disturbing images and dramatic language can amplify public anxiety and create a disproportionate fear of crime. For example, extensive reporting of one unusual attack may lead residents to avoid public transport even when no continuing threat exists. Coverage can also confer notoriety on offenders when it repeatedly displays their names, photographs or manifestos. This may encourage copycat behaviour among individuals seeking recognition. These risks justify limits on graphic material and unnecessary details about how crimes were committed, but they do not require all reporting to disappear.

A blanket prohibition would be excessive and potentially harmful. Accurate crime reporting can raise public awareness of genuine risks, distribute police warnings and encourage witnesses to provide information. It can also hold authorities to account by revealing failures in policing, sentencing or victim protection. Rather than suppressing the subject, media organisations should adopt stronger editorial safeguards: reports should provide context, protect victims' identities, avoid glorifying offenders and omit operational details that could facilitate imitation. Such responsible crime reporting would preserve the public's right to information while reducing the harmful effects associated with sensationalism.

In conclusion, violent-crime coverage can frighten audiences and occasionally encourage imitation, so tighter controls are warranted. Nevertheless, its public-information and accountability functions mean that regulation, rather than a total ban, is the more proportionate response.
