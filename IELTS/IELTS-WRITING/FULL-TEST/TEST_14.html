Overall, the company’s workforce shifted away from doctoral qualifications towards first and master’s degrees. Despite these changes within individual categories, the combined shares of science and arts graduates were identical in 1990 and 2010.

A5. Model Report — Band 7.5+

The two pie charts compare the percentages of graduates holding six different arts- and science-based qualifications who were employed by an engineering company in 1990 and 2010.

Overall, science qualifications accounted for a clear majority in both years, and the total division between science and arts remained unchanged at 63% and 37% respectively. Nevertheless, master’s degrees became more prevalent, while doctoral qualifications declined; Master’s Degree: Science remained the largest category.

Among science graduates, those with a master’s degree made up 27% in each year. The proportion holding a first degree rose from 18% to 24%, whereas science doctorates fell by an identical six percentage points, from 18% to 12%. This equal increase and decrease left the overall figure unchanged at 63%.

A similar redistribution occurred among arts graduates. Master’s Degree: Arts recorded the largest increase of any category, climbing from 15% to 23%. By contrast, First Degree: Arts declined from 10% to 7%, and Doctor’s Degree: Arts dropped from 12% to 7%. The rise in arts master’s graduates therefore offset the decline in the other arts categories, keeping their combined share at 37%. Across both fields, the combined share of master’s degrees increased from 42% to 50%, while doctorates decreased from 30% to 19%.

B4. Model Essay — Band 7.5+

Cities in different countries increasingly contain similar buildings, retail brands, transport systems and entertainment. This urban homogenisation is mainly caused by global business expansion, shared consumer preferences and the tendency to copy successful development models. Although greater uniformity can improve convenience and investment, I believe its cultural and local-economic costs are more significant.

The trend stems first from the spread of multinational retail chains and globally connected supply networks. Large companies normally reproduce the same store designs, products and service systems in every market, creating standardised commercial spaces. Consequently, a shopping street in one country may feature the same coffee shops, fashion outlets and restaurants as a city thousands of kilometres away. Urban authorities and developers also replicate successful urban models, including glass-tower business districts, metro systems and large shopping centres, because these appear efficient and commercially reliable. In addition, online media and international travel accelerate the international spread of tastes, encouraging residents to demand similar food, housing and leisure facilities.

This development has some benefits. Familiar payment systems, hotels and transport can offer a familiar environment to travellers, while international companies may attract foreign investment and expand employment and consumer choice. However, these gains do not compensate for the damage to local character. Standardised developments can erode local identity, replace traditional architecture and displace independent businesses that cannot compete with large chains. For example, converting a historic market into a generic shopping centre may remove both local livelihoods and a place where community traditions are practised. Such losses are long-lasting and difficult to reverse. Furthermore, when destinations become interchangeable, they may lose the distinctive appeal that supports cultural tourism.

In conclusion, cities are becoming alike because global companies, common tastes and copied planning models operate across national borders. While this provides convenience and economic access, the erosion of cultural identity and local enterprise affects whole communities for longer; therefore, the disadvantages outweigh the advantages.
