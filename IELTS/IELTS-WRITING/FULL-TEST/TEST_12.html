Introduction — one sentence

  • Original subject: Women’s representation in the workforce and management in five countries.

Overview — two sentences

Option A — Recommended

Overall, women were represented in management at roughly the same level as in the workforce in Australia and the United States, whereas substantial shortfalls were evident in the other three countries. Sri Lanka and Japan showed the widest gaps, while Australia and the US recorded the highest proportions of female managers.

Option B — Alternative

Overall, the share of women in the workforce varied considerably, but managerial representation was even more uneven. Female managers accounted for over two-fifths in Australia and the United States, compared with less than one-fifth in Egypt, Sri Lanka and Japan.

Best choice: Option A

A5. Model Report — Band 7.5+

The table compares the percentages of women in the overall workforce and in managerial positions in Australia, Egypt, Sri Lanka, Japan and the United States.

Overall, female representation in management closely matched workforce participation in Australia and the United States, whereas women were markedly under-represented in management in the other three countries. Sri Lanka and Japan displayed the widest disparities, while Australia and the US recorded the highest proportions of female managers.

In Australia, women accounted for 42% of the workforce, compared with 43% of managers, making it the only country where managerial representation was marginally higher. The corresponding figures for the United States were also close, at 46% and 43% respectively, which represented a gap of only three percentage points. Both countries therefore had 43% female managers.

By contrast, much larger differences were evident elsewhere. Japan had the highest workforce figure, at 48%, yet women held only 17% of managerial positions, producing a 31-point gap. The disparity was slightly greater in Sri Lanka, where 41% of workers were women but just 9% of managers were female. Egypt recorded the lowest workforce participation, at 20%, and its managerial figure stood at 12%, a comparatively modest shortfall of eight percentage points.

B4. Model Essay — Band 7.5+

Fast-food outlets and ready-made meals are increasingly replacing locally prepared dishes in many countries. Although this shift offers speed and affordability, I largely agree that it is harmful because regular reliance on fast food damages individual health and shared family meals, while also creating wider economic and cultural costs.

The most immediate effects are felt by individuals and households. Many fast-food products are energy-dense meals containing excessive salt, refined carbohydrates and unhealthy fats. Frequent consumption can therefore contribute to obesity and other diet-related illnesses, particularly when such meals replace balanced food prepared from fresh ingredients. For example, a busy family may repeatedly buy fried chicken or burgers after work, gradually making nutritionally poor choices their normal diet. The damage is not purely physical. Although fast food offers convenience, it may simultaneously undermine the routine of cooking and eating together. Shared meals give relatives time to communicate, and they allow children to learn recipes and food knowledge from older family members. If traditional meals are no longer prepared regularly, younger generations may lose both practical cooking skills and an important connection to family culture.

The effects also extend to society as a whole. A higher prevalence of preventable illness can place pressure on public healthcare and reduce productivity through absence from work. At the same time, the growth of large chains may displace independent food businesses, traditional markets and local producers. This can concentrate profits in multinational companies while weakening neighbourhood economies. Admittedly, fast-food firms provide employment and inexpensive meals, which can be valuable to low-income consumers. Nevertheless, these benefits are mainly practical and temporary. By contrast, the loss of local dishes and food-related customs can erode culinary heritage, and such cultural damage is difficult to reverse. Thus, the short-term benefits do not compensate for the long-term costs to public health and community identity.

In conclusion, I agree to a great extent that replacing traditional cuisine with fast food negatively affects individuals, families and society. Convenience and low prices are genuine advantages, but they are outweighed by poorer health, weaker family routines, pressure on public services and the gradual disappearance of local food culture.
